## 2. Why the exploration atmosphere moved to low pressure and higher O2

- The driver is frequent EVA. Lowering cabin N2 partial pressure reduces the O2 pre-breathe needed before a 4.3 psia suit EVA [H2, sec. 3.1, printed p.6; H6, printed p.24].
- Earlier proposed benefits also listed: lower inert-gas (N2) mass and lower vehicle internal pressure [H5, Introduction, printed p.1 / PDF p.5].
- The 2005 EAWG trade space for habitats and suits was hypoxia, flammability and DCS [H2, printed p.5]. A NASA risk briefing lists the atmosphere design considerations as: limit hypoxia, reduce risk of fire, prevent DCS, and allow efficient EVAs [H3, "Background" slide].
- Physiology vs fire: "Humans respond well to constant oxygen partial pressure (even at reduced total pressure)" while "Fire responds to total oxygen concentration" and "Fire risk sets the maximum oxygen boundary" [H6, printed p.24 / PDF p.25]. The 2005 study makes the same distinction: flammability "depends strongly on oxygen concentration (volume percent) and to a lesser extent on total pressure" [H1, sec. 2.3.1, printed p.11].
- Constellation-era baselines were 30% O2 maximum for Orion and 34% O2 maximum for the lunar lander (LSAM), "however, these numbers may change" [H4, PDF p.1]. A 2008 NASA memo gives Orion and Altair operating from 8.0 to 14.9 psia at a maximum of 30% and 34% O2 respectively [H5, printed p.1 / PDF p.5].

## 3. Fire-safety tradeoffs NASA documents

1. **More O2 by volume = more flammable.** "An enriched oxygen atmosphere also has a significant disadvantage--increased flammability of materials" [H5, Introduction, printed p.1]. Raising O2 concentration at constant pressure lowers minimum ignition energy, raises flame spread rate and increases the extinguishant needed [H1, sec. 2.3.1, printed pp.11–12].
2. **Fewer usable materials.** The number of self-extinguishing materials "falls off rapidly as the oxygen concentration is increased" [H1, sec. 2.3.1, printed p.14]; "Fire safe materials become increasingly difficult to find as the oxygen concentration increases" [H6, printed p.24].
3. **Materials database gap.** Most existing flammability data are from 30% O2 / 70.1 kPa Shuttle testing, so higher-O2, lower-pressure environments carry "unknown fire risks" [H4, PDF p.1]. NASA-STD-6001 Test 1 is run at the worst-case atmosphere of use, historically 30% O2 / 10.2 psia [H6, PDF p.27; H5, printed p.1].
4. **Pass/fail testing gives no margin.** Test 1 pass/fail logic "does not provide a measure of how far a material is removed from combustion threshold conditions" [H1, sec. 2.3.1, printed p.12]. NASA proposed measuring self-extinguishment limits (MOC) instead, partly to avoid retesting if the baseline O2 rises later [H4, PDF p.1].
5. **Conflict with radiation shielding and long missions.** The 2005 study notes hydrogen-rich polymers help radiation shielding, and long missions add flammable items (clothing, towels, food packaging, plant biomass, paper, filters); on flammability grounds alone this "would suggest a cabin oxygen concentration of Earth-normal (21%) or lower" [H1, secs. 2.3.2–2.3.4, printed pp.14–15]. That study then assumed a tentative 30% upper bound [H1, sec. 2.3.4, printed p.15]; the later 8/32 and 8.2/34 choices exceed that bound. Why the later groups accepted a higher value is given only as "acceptable flammability risk" [H2, printed p.5]; the underlying EAWG flammability analysis (NASA/TP-2010-216134, JSC-63309) was **not opened** in this review.
6. **Gravity matters; 1 g tests may not cover low gravity.** In drop-tower tests, 1 g ULOI and MOC were higher than 0 g values by up to 4% O2 mole fraction, meaning the fuels burned in 0 g at lower O2 than in Test 1 [H5, abstract, PDF p.5]. Partial gravity on the Moon or Mars "may therefore in some cases represent a more severe flammability environment" than 1 g or quiescent microgravity [H1, sec. 2.3.1, printed p.12]. No direct lunar-gravity burn data exist in the sources reviewed; any lunar estimate must be shown as a range between 0 g and 1 g results (see AGENTS.md).
7. **Ignition-source assumptions.** A JSC materials plan note reportedly calls a 25 W ignition threshold at 34% O2, 15 psia "incorrect and unconservative" (as summarized in `research/nasa-context.md`, JSC-27301 §5.1.1 g). **Not re-opened in this review.**
8. **Operational history.** Apollo-1 is listed as catastrophic; Mir and Shuttle fire events as non-catastrophic, attributed to materials controls, larger vehicles and much lower O2 [H3, flammability bullets].

## 4. Notes for REDLINE (inference, not NASA statements)

- Present 8.2 psia / 34% O2 as a **planned** atmosphere, with the 37% upper control-band value shown alongside it [H6].
- Do not convert 34% O2 at 56.5 kPa into an "equivalent" 1 g air result; fire tracks O2 concentration more than partial pressure [H1; H6], and pressure effects differ by material.
- Where a material has only 30% O2 / 70.1 kPa data, say "burned in testing at these conditions" or "close to a measured limit" for that condition; for 34% / 56.5 kPa say "not found in the sources reviewed".
